\begin{abstract}
Mikhail LeTal is a chess agent written for AI Chessathon 2026 by a two-person team. The shipped build
(v1.0, ``Stage 1'') carries its own board representation, move generation, evaluation and search,
compiled to machine code by numba at import; python-chess remains in the safety wrapper as the legality
oracle and the fallback. Every line of it is our own and is described here so that it can be explained.
The engine it replaced --- the same algorithms in plain Python on a python-chess board --- still ships
inside the zip, because it is the readable reference and the oracle the compiled engine is tested
against, integer for integer.

The compiled build beats that predecessor by \textbf{+560 Elo} (95\,\% interval +495 to +660; 300 games,
+279 =19 $-$2, 96.2\,\% $\pm$ 1.6\,\%) \src{RESULTS.md, row \code{numba-vs-v0.2-10s}}, reaching depth
10--14 in three seconds where it reached 6--10, at 691--913 thousand nodes per second against 50--69
thousand \src{RESULTS.md, ``Stage 1 phase 2'' tables}. It has been validated on the competition platform,
where it is measured at \textbf{depth 10--12} and 290--569 thousand nodes per second on the slower
competition core \src{CALIBRATION.md, 2026-09-08, v1.0 validation}. Against rating-limited Stockfish at
the full time control it scores 84.4\,\%, 75.0\,\%, 65.6\,\% and 56.2\,\% at UCI\_Elo 1800, 2000, 2200 and
2400, 16 games each \src{RESULTS.md, rows \code{v1.0-vs-sf*-real}}; those brackets are wide and no
CCRL-scale rating is claimed for this build (Section~\ref{sec:rating}).

No game in any of those matches ended in a crash, an illegal move, a flag fall or a start-up failure.
Every number in this document is a measurement quoted from \code{docs/RESULTS.md} or
\code{docs/CALIBRATION.md}, or a constant quoted from the code and its provenance files; where a figure
is an estimate rather than a measurement it says so. Two measurements remain open and are marked in red
throughout.
\end{abstract}

\section{The competition and what was built}
\paragraph{Format.} AI Chessathon 2026 is a competition for chess agents written by student teams. A
team uploads a zip whose root \code{agent.py} exposes one function, \code{get\_move(fen, time\_left\_ms)},
returning a move in UCI notation (for example \code{e2e4}, or \code{e7e8q} for a promotion). The platform
plays the latest build that passed validation on a rated ladder (hourly rounds, 08:00--22:00 London). The
ladder only seeds a 13-round Swiss tournament over locked builds on the afternoon of Friday
11~September; the Swiss decides qualification for the London final on Saturday 12~September, where each
finalist team walks a judge through how its agent was built. Uploads close at 11:00 London on
11~September.

\paragraph{Clock and machine.} Each side has 120~s plus 0.5~s per move, measured in wall time by the
runner from the moment it writes the request to the moment it reads the reply, so process wake-up, FEN
parsing and board construction are on our clock. The agent runs as one process per game, suspended while
the opponent thinks, on one core of an AMD EPYC 9V74 at 2.60~GHz with 2~GB of RAM, no network and no
GPU; it gets 90~s at start-up before the clock runs. The Python environment is fixed: Python 3.12 with
\code{python-chess 1.11.2}, \code{numpy 2.5.2}, \code{numba 0.67.0}, \code{torch 2.13.0} (CPU) and
\code{onnxruntime 1.29.0}; nothing else can be installed and native binaries are rejected. A game is lost
outright on an illegal move, a crash, running out of memory, missing the 90~s start-up budget, or a flag
fall (unless the opponent cannot mate, which is a draw). Draws follow FIDE rules and the referee claims
threefold repetition and fifty-move draws automatically; a game that reaches 600 plies (half-moves,
counting the opening position) is a draw. Every game starts from a curated near-level opening position,
not from the initial position, and the set of openings is not published \src{brief, \S 3; \code{AGENTS.md}}.

\paragraph{What was built.} The shipped build is v1.0, Stage 1 of a staged plan
(Section~\ref{sec:arch}). It ships \code{agent.py} (the wrapper) and the package \code{mikhail\_letal}:
the compiled board, evaluation and search (\code{fastboard}, \code{fasteval}, \code{fastsearch}), the
Python reference engine they are tested against (\code{evaluation}, \code{search}, \code{searchboard}),
and the timing, game-state, warm-up and fallback modules --- plus \code{weights/pst.json} and
\code{weights/PROVENANCE.json}. Fourteen files, 85\,925 bytes zipped and 269\,589 unzipped, against a
50~MB cap. On the development machine it reaches depth 10--14 with selective depth 26--30 in three
seconds, at 691--913 thousand nodes per second \src{RESULTS.md, ``Stage 1 phase 2'' tables}; on the
competition core, depth 10--12 \src{CALIBRATION.md, 2026-09-08, v1.0 validation}.

\paragraph{Our result.} Local: 200/200 against \code{baselines/random} by checkmate with zero failure
terminations, and +560 Elo over 300 games against the previous shipped build (Section~\ref{sec:testing}
and the v1.0 addendum). Against rating-limited Stockfish~19 at the real clock, 16 games per level: 84.4\,\%
at UCI\_Elo 1800, 75.0\,\% at 2000, 65.6\,\% at 2200 and 56.2\,\% at 2400, no flag at any level
\src{RESULTS.md, rows \code{v1.0-vs-sf*-real}}. The predecessor v0.1 cleared the brief's Stage 0 bar --- a
score of at least 90\,\% against \code{baselines/minimax} with zero flags --- at 98.8\,\% ($\pm$2.4\,\%)
over 40 real-clock games \src{RESULTS.md, row \code{v0.1-vs-minimax-real-clock}}, and the solo per-move
pass in Section~\ref{sec:testing} shows the time budget holding, with a worst overshoot of 2~ms.

On the platform: v1.0 passed validation, reaching depth 10--12 in both smoke games with every warm-up
phase completed \src{CALIBRATION.md, 2026-09-08, v1.0 validation}.

\paragraph{The ladder rating, and why little should be read into it.} At the snapshot taken at 19:44
on 8~September, v1.0 stood at a provisional ladder rating of \textbf{1669} from \textbf{five} rated
games, won 3, drawn 1, lost 1 \src{data/cache/leaderboard/qualifier.html}. The leaders in the same
table hold 2573--2763 on \textbf{48--65} games. Five games is an unconverged seed rather than a
rating, and the snapshot was already stale when it was taken: two further rated games (rounds 74 and
75) have since been played and are not in it. The ladder also decides nothing on its own --- it seeds
the 13-round Swiss on 11~September, and only the Swiss counts for qualification. The number is
recorded here because a report that omits its own worst-looking figure is not a record; it is not
offered as a measurement of strength, and the Stockfish brackets in Section~\ref{sec:rating} are the
better estimate.

\section{Rules-compliance statement}\label{sec:rules}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item No third-party engine, wrapper, port or translation is shipped or consulted at runtime. The
        libraries used at runtime are \code{python-chess 1.11.2}, \code{numba 0.67} and \code{numpy 2.5},
        all part of the platform's fixed stack. python-chess supplies board representation and the rules
        in the safety wrapper; numba and numpy supply compilation and array storage. None of them supplies
        chess knowledge: the board representation, move generation, evaluation and search that play the
        game are ours.
  \item \textbf{No native binary ships.} numba compiles from the Python source in the zip, at import, on
        the platform's own machine. What is uploaded is source a judge can read, which is what the rules
        require; nothing is precompiled, and \code{cache=True} is not used --- the platform's filesystem is
        read-only apart from \code{/tmp}, which is wiped between games, so a cache would buy nothing.
  \item No published network is shipped; this build ships no network at all. No parameter that ships was
        fitted to positions labelled by another engine. Texel tuning was attempted and rejected on
        measurement, so the evaluation that ships is the untuned parametric prior
        (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
  \item No lookup database of another engine's moves or evaluations ships in the zip. There is no opening
        book and no tablebase in this build (Section~\ref{sec:endgame}).
  \item Memorised-code risk. An engine written with a language model can reproduce code or tables it has
        seen, and Sunfish, whose tables are famous, is a house bot on this ladder. Three measures were
        taken. (1)~Every evaluation table is generated by \code{tools/gen\_pst.py} from a parametric
        geometric prior (Section~\ref{sec:eval}): each entry is a formula of the square's geometry times a
        named parameter, so the numbers are ours by construction and re-running the script reproduces
        them bit for bit. (2)~Search and evaluation were written against a module contract
        (\code{docs/DESIGN.md}) from textbook descriptions of the algorithms, and a review pass looked for
        surface overlaps with known engines: the one found was the class-and-method pair
        \code{Searcher.search}, which Sunfish also uses; our class was renamed \code{Engine} even though
        everything behind it differs \src{DECISIONS.md, 2026-09-07 item F}. (3)~The generated tables were
        compared mechanically against the published tables of \textbf{Sunfish}, the Chess Programming
        Wiki's \textbf{``simplified evaluation function''}, \textbf{Rustic Alpha 3}, \textbf{TSCP} and
        \textbf{VICE}: whole-table comparisons and affine comparisons (does any table of ours equal a
        published one after multiplying by a constant and adding a constant?) were all clean, and a
        row-by-row comparison found exactly \textbf{one coincidental row}: the endgame pawn table's
        seventh rank is 50 in both ours and the CPW table, because our formula gives
        $14 \times 5^2 / 7 = 50$ there \src{DECISIONS.md, 2026-09-07 item I}.
  \item Local measuring instruments. A rating-limited Stockfish (path read from the environment variable
        \code{YARDSTICK\_ENGINE}) and the real Sunfish are used only as local sparring partners for the
        rating estimate (Section~\ref{sec:rating}); they live outside the repository, never enter the zip,
        never influence a move, and their source was not read to inform the design.
  \item Provenance. Every constant that ships has a row in Appendix~\ref{app:provenance}, copied from
        \code{docs/PROVENANCE.md} and \code{weights/PROVENANCE.json}, which itself ships in the zip.
  \item Everything shipped is plain, type-annotated Python that passes \code{ruff check} and
        \code{mypy --strict} \src{INTEGRATION\_NOTES.md, ``Checks run''}. No generated table ships without
        its generating script; nothing is minified or obfuscated.
\end{itemize}

\section{Architecture and why}\label{sec:arch}
The plan has stages, and each stage replaces the previous shipped build only after beating it under the
real clock with a 95\,\% interval above zero. Three architectures were on the table.

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{python-chess board + our own search and evaluation in pure Python} (Stage 0, builds
        v0.1 and v0.2). python-chess is a proven implementation of the rules, so correctness risk is
        confined to our own code: search, evaluation, time management. This is what the first priority
        of the brief (never lose a game for free) demands from the first session. It is no longer the
        shipped architecture, but it still ships inside the zip as the reference implementation and as
        the oracle the compiled engine is tested against (Section~\ref{sec:testing}).
  \item \textbf{Own board representation, move generation, evaluation and search compiled with numba}
        (Stage 1, \textbf{this build}), with python-chess kept in the wrapper as the legality oracle and
        fallback. numba compiles at every game start, so the compile time is paid at import, inside the
        90~s start-up budget and before the clock starts: measured at 18.5~s from the extracted zip
        \src{RESULTS.md, row ``import + compile, extracted zip''}. The root of the iterative-deepening
        loop is deliberately left in Python; only \code{negamax} crosses into compiled code, once per
        root move per iteration. Compiling the root as well was measured and rejected: it raised total
        compile time to 34.2~s for no gain, because the root executes a handful of times per move rather
        than millions \src{RESULTS.md, rows ``compile time if the root is compiled too'' and ``Python
        $\rightarrow$ jitted call boundary''}.
  \item \textbf{A small self-trained NNUE-style network} evaluated inside the jitted search (Stage 4,
        expected to be skipped): \code{onnxruntime} cannot be called once per node from a jitted search,
        training data would have to be produced first, and the operator would rather explain a tuned
        hand-written evaluation than a rushed network.
\end{enumerate}

A fourth option, jitting only the evaluation under a python-chess board, was rejected by arithmetic on the
measured numbers: at 50--61 thousand nodes per second one node costs roughly 16--20~$\mu$s, of which
\code{evaluate()} is 3.4--3.8~$\mu$s in the middlegame and 1.6~$\mu$s in the endgame \src{RESULTS.md, row
``\code{evaluate()} cost''}, so the evaluation is at most a fifth of the node cost and numba cannot read
python-chess objects without a conversion that would cost more than it saves. The speed has to come from
replacing the board and the move generator, which is Stage 1.

Under Stage 0, import time was measured three times by timing \code{import agent} with
\code{time.perf\_counter()} in a fresh interpreter and was 0.033~s each time, including a depth-2 warm-up

\paragraph{What import means once the engine is compiled.} Under Stage 1 that 0.033~s becomes 18.5~s,
and the warm-up stops being a formality. numba compiles a function the first time it is called with a
given argument type, and it cannot be interrupted: a function left uncompiled until the game is running
would compile \emph{inside} a search, blowing straight through the hard deadline that protects the clock.
So every jitted function is listed in its module and called once at import, on positions chosen to
trigger the same type signatures the game will, and \code{tests/test\_fasteval.py} asserts that no new
signature appears afterwards under load --- a second signature would mean a compilation deferred onto the
clock. The warm-up also carries a wall-clock deadline, so that an unexpectedly slow container degrades
into a shallower first move rather than losing every game: if the budget runs out, the remaining
compilation is finished on the first real move, on the fullest clock of the game. In v1.0 that finish
is itself bounded, by \code{cold\_finish\_fraction} of the clock.

That bound was later found not to hold. The budget is re-armed for the cold finish, and re-arming
resets the slowdown measured during the import --- the very number that made the import skip phases in
the first place --- so the remaining work was predicted at development-machine speed on a machine
already known to be slower. The bound has been removed rather than repaired: everything left is
compiled unconditionally, and the cost is subtracted from the clock before the move's budget is
computed, so the search that follows shrinks to fit it. Between an accounted cost and an unaccounted
one, the accounted one is preferable --- a phase deferred instead would compile inside
\code{ENGINE.search}, where numba cannot be interrupted and no deadline applies. \textbf{That change is
not in v1.0}, the build this report describes; it is recorded here because the bound v1.0 ships with is
weaker than it appears.

\section{Board representation and move generation}
\paragraph{What the shipped build uses.} v1.0 has its own board and its own move generator:
\code{mikhail\_letal/fastboard.py}, a \textbf{0x88 mailbox} representation compiled by numba. A 0x88 board
stores the 64 squares in a 128-entry array whose spare half makes the off-board test a single bit
operation --- \code{square \& 0x88} is non-zero exactly when a square is off the edge --- which removes the
bounds checking that dominates a naive mailbox generator and suits numba, where an array index is cheap
and an exception is not. Moves are packed into integers rather than objects, positions are identified by
our own Zobrist keys (\code{fastboard.position\_key}), and make/unmake restores the position in place
rather than copying it. Correctness is established by perft and legal-move-set agreement against
python-chess, described in Section~\ref{sec:testing}; because the generator is ours, that agreement is a
real test rather than a tautology.

python-chess remains in \code{agent.py}, where it converts the incoming FEN, supplies the legality check
that every returned move passes, and provides the fallback. So the rules of chess are still enforced by
the library at the boundary, and the speed comes from our own representation inside it.

\paragraph{What Stage 0 used, and why the two are described together.} The interpreted engine, which still
ships as the reference implementation and the oracle, uses python-chess's \code{Board} throughout: a
bitboard representation (one 64-bit integer per piece type
and per colour) with fully legal move generation, written and tested by the library's authors and pinned
by the platform at version 1.11.2. Quiescence uses \code{Board.generate\_legal\_captures()} so that only
captures are generated at the leaves. Squares are indexed as python-chess does (\code{a1 = 0},
\code{h1 = 7}, \code{a8 = 56}); evaluation tables are stored from White's point of view and a Black piece
on square $s$ reads the table at $s \oplus 56$ (a vertical mirror). Positions are identified by
python-chess's own repetition key, \code{Board.\_transposition\_key()}, a tuple of the piece bitboards, the
side to move, the castling rights and the en passant square; it is exactly what \code{Board.is\_repetition}
uses, so what we count as a repetition is what the referee counts \src{DECISIONS.md, 2026-09-06,
``Position keys''}. Two costs are accepted knowingly: the key is a private name (an upgrade of the library
could remove it, but the platform pins 1.11.2), and it is a tuple rather than a Zobrist hash. The compiled
board replaces it with its own Zobrist keys, so nothing on the path that plays a rated move depends on a
private name.

\paragraph{Perft.} A perft test counts the leaf nodes of the full move tree to a fixed depth and compares
the count with a reference; it is the standard proof that a move generator is correct. It did not apply to
Stage 0, which had no move generator of its own: legality agreement with the referee, which also used
python-chess, held by construction. It applies squarely to the shipped build, whose generator is ours, and
Section~\ref{sec:testing} gives the four gates that establish it. The evidence that the surrounding code uses
the library correctly is the property-test set (each of the following returns a legal move within budget:
all four promotion pieces, capture-promotions, under-promotion to a knight when it is the only mate, en
passant for both colours, castling both ways for both colours, a stalemate trap, insufficient material,
a single legal move, mate in one, halfmove clock 99, a would-be third repetition) and the 500 fuzz games
with zero illegal moves (Section~\ref{sec:testing}) \src{tests/test\_properties.py; RESULTS.md fuzz rows}.
The Stage 1 move generator will be perft-tested against python-chess to depth 4 on the standard public
positions and to depth 3 on every collected opening and on at least 2\,000 random playout positions, plus a
legal-move-set equality test on at least 100\,000 random positions, before it replaces the library
\src{brief, \S 5 Stage 1 DoD}; that table will be added here.

\section{Search}\label{sec:search}
\paragraph{The algorithm in one paragraph.} The search is a \emph{negamax} tree search: every score is
measured from the side to move's point of view, so the value of a position is the maximum over moves of
the negated value of the resulting position. \emph{Alpha-beta pruning} carries a window $(\alpha, \beta)$
of scores that can still matter to the ancestors and abandons a child whose value falls outside it.
\emph{Iterative deepening} runs the search to depth 1, then 2, then 3, and so on until the clock says
stop; each iteration seeds the next with good first-try moves, and a fully searched answer is always
available. At the leaves a \emph{quiescence} search continues with captures (and queen promotions) only,
because a static evaluation in the middle of an exchange is meaningless; the side to move may always
\emph{stand pat}, that is, accept the static evaluation instead of capturing. A \emph{transposition table}
(TT) remembers, per position key, the depth, score, score type (exact, lower bound or upper bound) and
best move of an earlier visit, so that positions reached by different move orders are not searched
twice. Scores are integers in centipawns (hundredths of a pawn); a mate delivered $p$ plies from the root
scores $100\,000 - p$, so shorter mates score higher. All of this is in \code{mikhail\_letal/search.py}.

\paragraph{Why these techniques and not others here.} The platform gives one slow core and pure Python
gives tens of thousands of nodes per second, so the tree is small (about $10^5$--$10^6$ nodes per move at
the real clock) and every technique that makes a small tree go deeper, or that keeps the clock check
honest, matters more than it would at millions of nodes per second. Null-move pruning, late-move
reductions, futility pruning, aspiration windows and quiescence delta pruning were deliberately absent from
Stage 0, so that each could be added as a separate experiment rather than as an undifferentiated block
\src{DECISIONS.md, 2026-09-06, ``No null-move pruning''}. All of them were added in v0.2 and all of them
ship in v1.0; the v0.2 addendum lists the constants each was given, and the combined effect was to cut the
nodes needed to reach depth 6 from the standard start from 238\,000 to 17\,000.

\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}
\paragraph{Terms.} The static evaluation (\code{mikhail\_letal/evaluation.py}) has three terms: material,
piece-square tables and a mop-up term. It is \emph{tapered}: every piece has a middlegame table and an
endgame table, and the position's \emph{phase} blends them. The phase counts the non-pawn pieces on the
board with weights knight 1, bishop 1, rook 2, queen 4, so it is 24 with every piece on the board and 0
with only kings and pawns; the score is
\[
  \mathrm{score} = \frac{\mathrm{mg} \cdot \mathrm{phase} + \mathrm{eg} \cdot (24 - \mathrm{phase})}{24},
\]
truncated toward zero so that mirrored positions score exactly opposite. Material values are folded into
the tables at load time, so the hot loop does one table lookup per piece per phase. Insufficient material
scores 0. The result is negated for Black to move, so the search sees every node the same way. Piece
bitboards are read directly rather than through \code{pieces\_mask} and \code{scan\_forward} (same
semantics, 1.7$\times$ faster) \src{INTEGRATION\_NOTES.md, ``Builder deviations''}.

\paragraph{Piece values.} Pawn 100, knight 320, bishop 330, rook 500, queen 900, in both phases. These
are the universal textbook values, recorded as such and untuned \src{weights/PROVENANCE.json}.

\paragraph{The parametric prior.} Each of the twelve tables (six pieces $\times$ two phases) is computed
by \code{tools/gen\_pst.py} from the square's geometry. Two helper quantities: \emph{centrality} is
$3 - \max(|\mathrm{file} - 3.5|, |\mathrm{rank} - 3.5|)$, which is $2.5$ on the four centre squares and
$-0.5$ on the rim; \emph{advancement} is the rank seen from the piece's own side, 0 to 7. Then: pawns gain
\code{pawn\_adv\_mg} per step forward and \code{pawn\_centre\_mg} on d/e files on ranks 3--5 in the
middlegame, and \code{pawn\_adv\_eg}$\cdot \mathrm{advancement}^2/7$ in the endgame (a passed-pawn shape);
knights gain \code{knight\_centre} $\times$ centrality and lose \code{knight\_rim} on the outer ring;
bishops gain \code{bishop\_centre} $\times$ centrality and \code{bishop\_long\_diagonal} on the two long
diagonals; rooks gain \code{rook\_seventh} on the seventh rank and \code{rook\_centre\_file} on d/e files
in the middlegame and are flat in the endgame; queens gain \code{queen\_centre} $\times$ centrality
$\times 0.5$ and lose \code{queen\_early} for leaving the back two ranks in the middlegame; the king gains
\code{king\_shelter} on b1/c1/g1 and loses \code{king\_centre\_penalty} per rank of advancement and on the
d/e files in the middlegame, and gains \code{king\_centre\_eg} $\times$ centrality in the endgame. The
script prints every table as an 8$\times$8 grid for a human to read, records the parameters and the
tables' SHA-256 in the JSON's \code{\_provenance} object, and a test checks that the shipped file matches
the generator bit for bit (\code{test\_pst\_json\_matches\_the\_generator}).

\paragraph{Mop-up.} When one side has a bare king, the other has no pawns and at least a rook's worth of
material (or two minor pieces), the stronger side gains
$\code{mopup\_edge} \cdot d_{\mathrm{centre}}(\text{weak king}) + \code{mopup\_close} \cdot (14 -
d(\text{king}, \text{king}))$, with $d$ the Manhattan distance: drive the bare king to the edge and bring
ours close. The idea is textbook geometry; the two weights (10 and 12) are ours and were chosen by hand
so that ``close'' beats one ring of the endgame king table \src{DECISIONS.md, 2026-09-06, ``Mop-up term''}.

\paragraph{Cost.} \code{evaluate()} takes 3.4--3.8~$\mu$s in the middlegame and 1.6~$\mu$s in the endgame
(10\,000-call loops) \src{RESULTS.md, row ``\code{evaluate()} cost''}.

\paragraph{How the weights were obtained: tuning was tried and rejected.} The shipped weights are the
parametric prior as generated, untuned. That is not because tuning was skipped --- it was run, and it
lost.

Texel-style tuning of the piece values and tables was implemented in \code{tools/tune\_texel.py} and
measured against the untuned set under the ordinary promotion rule. The first attempt scored 36.0\,\% over
300 games, an interval of $-100$ Elo ($-140$ to $-62$), comfortably \emph{below} zero. A second attempt
with stronger regularisation recovered most of the loss but still did not clear the bar: 46.7\,\%, $-23$
Elo ($-61$ to $+14$) \src{RESULTS.md, rows \code{v0.3-texel-vs-v0.2-10s} and
\code{v0.3-texel-lambda40-vs-v0.2-10s}}. Under a rule that promotes only on an interval above zero, the
untuned prior stands.

Both runs are kept in \code{RESULTS.md} rather than deleted. The most likely reading is over-fitting: the
tuning set is small next to the number of free parameters in twelve 64-entry tables, and a prior built
from a geometric formula generalises better than weights fitted to a few thousand positions. It is
recorded here as a negative result because a report that lists only what worked is not evidence of a
method.

\section{Time management}\label{sec:time}
\paragraph{The budget formula.} Every constant lives in one frozen dataclass, \code{TimeParams}
(file \code{mikhail\_letal/timing.py}), so that a calibration changes one object and nothing else. With
$t$ the clock in milliseconds before the move and $m$ the number of moves we have played this game:
\begin{align*}
  \mathrm{moves\_to\_go} &= \mathrm{clamp}(50 - \lfloor 0.7\,m \rfloor,\ 20,\ 50), \\
  \mathrm{moves\_to\_go} &= \min(\mathrm{moves\_to\_go},\ \lceil \mathrm{plies\_to\_cap}/2 \rceil,\ \lceil \mathrm{fifty\_move\_room}/2 \rceil), \\
  \mathrm{soft} &= (t - \mathrm{overhead}) / \mathrm{moves\_to\_go} + 0.8 \times 500, \\
  \mathrm{hard} &= \min(3 \times \mathrm{soft},\ 0.25\, t), \\
  \mathrm{floor} &= \max(1500,\ 0.05\, t), \\
  \mathrm{hard} &= \min(\mathrm{hard},\ t - \mathrm{overhead} - \mathrm{floor}), \qquad \mathrm{soft} = \min(\mathrm{soft}, \mathrm{hard}),
\end{align*}
both clamped to be non-negative. \code{plies\_to\_cap} $= 600 - \mathrm{ply}$ is always passed;
\code{fifty\_move\_room} $= 100 - \mathrm{halfmove\ clock}$ is passed only in a pawnless mop-up, where
nothing but the mate itself resets the halfmove clock (the urgency clamps of review item C). Worked
examples from the unit tests: at $t = 120\,000$, $m = 0$: moves\_to\_go 50, soft $= 119\,950/50 + 400 =
2799$~ms, hard $= 8397$~ms; at $t = 60\,000$, $m = 20$: moves\_to\_go 36, soft $= 59\,950/36 + 400 \approx
2065$~ms, hard $\approx 6196$~ms \src{tests/test\_timing.py}.

\paragraph{Where the divisor comes from.} $50$, $20$ and $0.7$ are not chosen by feel. 1\,697 finished
ladder games were collected and used to measure how many of \emph{our} moves are really left at each
point of a game, given the game reached that point: the median is 67 at the start, falls by about one a
move to roughly 25 by move 50, and then flattens. If a move spent its whole soft budget, sharing the clock
over that curve would be the right answer directly. It does not --- a move spends about 0.70 of its soft
budget --- so the divisor is that curve scaled by 0.70, which makes the \emph{realised} spending an even
split of the clock over the moves that are really left. Hence $0.7 \times 67 \approx 47 \rightarrow 50$ at
the start, and a decay of 0.7 divisor-moves per move played.

The floor is the interesting one, and it is set by a safety argument rather than by the median. At full
spend the clock settles where a move's cost equals the increment, at $\mathrm{overhead} +
\mathrm{moves\_to\_go\_min} \times 500 \times (1/\mathrm{usage} - 0.8)$. At a floor of 16 that lands on
$1650$~ms --- \emph{exactly} \code{panic\_ms}, the threshold below which the engine is skipped entirely,
so the tail of a long game would settle precisely on the boundary where it stops searching. At 20 it is
$2050$~ms at full spend and about 6.3~s at the measured 0.70, and over the ladder games that ran to at
least 90 of our moves the lowest clock reached rises from 4.6~s to 5.9~s. The floor is therefore 20.

\paragraph{How the budget is used.} \code{get\_move} starts its own timer on entry, so board construction
and history bookkeeping count against the budget as they do on the platform. The searcher is handed two
absolute deadlines: the soft budget as a target, and a hard deadline at which the search aborts
mid-iteration, with the clock read every 512 nodes in the compiled search (Section~\ref{sec:search}).
Below \code{panic\_ms} $= 1650$~ms the engine is skipped and the fallback plays
(Section~\ref{sec:safety}); a position with a single legal move is played without searching.

\paragraph{Starting the next depth: a prediction, not a fixed share.} Whether to begin depth $d+1$ used to
be decided by a fixed fraction of the budget --- start one only before $0.45 \times \mathrm{soft}$ had
elapsed. The platform's own logs showed what that does. Per-move times came in two lumps and nothing
between: 1.5--3.0~s, where an iteration finished and the engine stopped with a third of its budget unspent,
or 8.9--10.1~s, where it started a depth that could not finish and ran into the hard ceiling. The cause is
arithmetic. Iteration costs grow geometrically --- each depth costs about 4--5 times the previous --- so a
depth begun at 0.45 of the budget cannot finish inside it, and a fixed fraction has no way to tell the two
cases apart.

The rule now predicts. The cost of depth $d+1$ is estimated as $\rho \times T(d)$, where $\rho$ is
measured from the last two completed iterations, clamped to $[2.0, 8.0]$ and defaulting to 4.5 before
there is data; the depth is started only if it is predicted to end inside a target. The target is the soft
budget times \code{iteration\_target\_factor}, stretched by half again when the best root move changed at
the last completed depth, and cut to 0.7 when it has been stable for six iterations with a score that is
not falling. Insisting that the next depth \emph{finish} by the soft budget is the obvious reading and the
wrong one: with geometric costs it stops a factor of $\rho$ short and leaves a third of the clock unspent,
which is why the target factor is 1.35 rather than 1.0.

Every target is clamped to the hard window, so the guarantee that matters is preserved by construction: a
decision to start an iteration implies the elapsed time is still inside the hard deadline. The abort path
and the flag invariant are untouched, and \code{tests/test\_timing.py} asserts the guarantee over a grid
of histories and windows. The old fixed fraction survives only as a fallback for an iteration too short to
measure (under 1~ms), where a ratio would be meaningless.

\paragraph{Calibration procedure.} When a validation log arrives the operator pastes it and the following
is done \src{CALIBRATION.md, ``Procedure''}: (1)~extract the init time, every per-move time, the clock left
after each move and any stderr; (2)~pair each platform-measured move time with the self-measured \code{t}
token of our log line; the difference is the platform overhead for that move, and
\code{overhead\_ms} $= \max(\text{difference}) + 50$; (3)~compute the platform node rate from our \code{nps}
tokens and divide by the local node rate on the same build and positions to get the speed factor, and
rescale any node cap; (4)~record the platform init time against the local one; (5)~commit the new
constants with the log excerpt. \textbf{That procedure has been run.} The interpreted v0.2.1's rated
logs of 8~September gave the 2.3x speed factor and the 0--2~ms per-move overhead that set
\code{overhead\_ms}; v1.0's own validation and its rated rounds gave the start-up figures and the
depth and node rates quoted in the v1.0 addendum; and round 74 supplied a full per-move table for a
whole rated game \src{CALIBRATION.md, ``Log of calibrations''; handoff/LOG-round-74-zagreus.md}. Every
time constant in Section~\ref{sec:time} is calibrated against those measurements rather than against
the brief's initial values.

The one part of the procedure that has *not* converged is start-up. Six samples of the compiled
build's import range over 33.2--49.4~s of numba compilation against a 90~s budget, and the five fast
ones are all uncontended single rated games while the slow one is half of a concurrent validation
pair --- so the samples do not yet bound the case where two of our games compile at once, which the
agent contract permits. That is recorded as the build's largest open risk in the addendum rather than
resolved here.

a Stage 2 experiment; it ships only if the files fit inside the size cap with margin and an arena run from
endgame-heavy positions shows a positive interval, otherwise the mop-up term stays
\src{DECISIONS.md, 2026-09-06, ``Mop-up term instead of tablebases''}.
\paragraph{Draw handling.} Inside the tree the terminal checks run in this order: ply $\geq 600$ is a draw;
a position already in the game history or on the search path is a draw; halfmove clock $\geq 100$ is
checkmate if the side to move is mated, otherwise a draw (the referee checks mate first, and so do we:

received, as the referee's does, and is kept by \code{GameState} (Section~\ref{sec:safety}). Because any
earlier occurrence scores a draw, the engine steers away from a repetition before the third occurrence
when ahead, and toward one when behind. Near a rule deadline the urgency clamps
(Section~\ref{sec:time}) and the root tie-break (Section~\ref{sec:search}) make sure a won position is not
shuffled into a draw.

the count matches the referee's, which counts the opening position \src{tests: \code{test\_ply\_cap\_scores\_draw}}.

\section{Safety and failure handling}\label{sec:safety}
\paragraph{The wrapper.} \code{agent.py} is deliberately small. \code{get\_move} builds the board from
the FEN, updates the game history, lists the legal moves, decides a budget, calls the engine, and checks
the answer. The whole body is inside one \code{try}: any exception is logged in one line and the fallback
plays. The engine's move is accepted only if it is in the legal-move list; then a \emph{final guard}
(\code{\_validated}) parses the UCI string back and looks it up in the legal moves of a fresh
\code{chess.Board(fen)}, exactly as the referee will, replacing it with the fallback if that fails. Only
after this check is the move recorded in the history and printed. Even the logging is wrapped, and lines
are capped at 120 bytes because the platform keeps only the first and last 4~KB of output.
\paragraph{The fallback.} \code{fallback\_move} looks one ply ahead: a mate in one if there is one,
otherwise the move that leaves us the most material, preferring big captures and queen promotions, with the
UCI string as the last tie-break so the same position always gives the same move. It returns in a few
milliseconds and is tested on its own (mate in one, best capture, promotion, en passant, determinism,
legality in random positions, speed) \src{tests/test\_fallback.py}.
\paragraph{The panic band.} Below \code{panic\_ms} $= 1650$~ms the engine is not called at all. The
threshold equals \code{overhead\_ms} $+$ \code{floor\_ms}: in the band 1500--1650~ms the formula returned
a zero budget and the search aborted at its first clock check with a depth-0 move, so the band was routed
to the fallback instead \src{INTEGRATION\_NOTES.md, ``For the reviewers'' item 2; commit 974264d}.
\paragraph{Desync reset.} The platform sends nothing but a FEN and a clock. \code{GameState.observe}
expects each received position to be one legal move away from the position after our last move, and
reconstructs the opponent's move by trying every legal move and comparing repetition keys. If no move
matches (a desync: the platform and our history disagree), the history is reset to the received position,
a line is logged, and the engine's transposition table is cleared, because stored draws may rest on the
history just discarded \src{agent.py; DECISIONS.md item E}. One known gap is benign by design: when we
play Black we never see the game's true start position, so its count stays one below the referee's; since
any earlier occurrence already scores a draw, nothing changes
(\code{test\_black\_never\_sees\_the\_start\_position}).
\paragraph{Start-up.} A depth-2 search from the initial position runs at import so every code path has
executed before the clock starts; the table is cleared after it. \code{OMP\_NUM\_THREADS} and
\code{NUMBA\_NUM\_THREADS} are set to 1 before any other import. Log lines are printed with
\code{flush=True}: the runner points our stdout at a pipe, which Python block-buffers, and kills the
process when the game ends; without the flush the first local game printed no agent output at all
\src{INTEGRATION\_NOTES.md, ``What changed in integration''}.
\paragraph{Fuzz counts.} 300 games against \code{baselines/random} at 3~s + 0.05~s and 200 against
\code{baselines/greedy} at 10~s + 0.1~s, over the collected openings, twelve games in parallel: 500 games,
500 wins by checkmate, zero terminations by crash, illegal move, flag or init
\src{RESULTS.md, rows \code{fuzz-random-v0.1} and \code{fuzz-greedy-v0.1}}. The random mover is the
opponent that plays the strange moves (under-promotions, en passant, stalemate tries) that exercise the
guard paths.

The compiled engine was fuzzed the same way: 200 games against \code{baselines/random}, 200 wins by
checkmate, zero failure terminations \src{RESULTS.md, row \code{numba-fuzz-random}}. A re-run on the final
build was started and \textbf{stopped at 25 games of 200} to give the machine to a measured match, and is
recorded as stopped rather than quietly dropped: 25 games, 25 wins by checkmate, no failed termination,
lowest clock 1\,649~ms of 3\,000. The final build's legality argument therefore rests on the full test
suite --- which includes a legal move being required from the compiled search on 3\,000 sampled positions
at three node limits, and the property suite driving \code{agent.get\_move} --- rather than on a completed
200-game fuzz. That is a real gap and it is stated as one: a gate that was not finished should not be
read as one that was.

\section{Rating estimate}\label{sec:rating}
This section is a method statement, and it now describes two builds. \textbf{The rating below belongs to
the interpreted v0.2, not to the shipped v1.0.}

\paragraph{v0.2 (interpreted), fully measured.} 300 real-clock games against Stockfish~19 at UCI\_Elo
1600--2400 on 8~September (60 per level, six games at a time; scores 70.0, 76.7, 45.0, 38.3 and 43.3\,\%;
zero flags): CCRL-40/4-scale $\approx$ 2050 (95\,\% interval 1940--2170: bootstrap 2000--2110 plus
$\pm$100 yardstick calibration in quadrature); FIDE-equivalent $\approx$ 1840--2360 (Route A:
CCRL$-$100 as the lower bound, TalkChess $2800 - 0.7 \times (2800 - \mathrm{CCRL})$ as the upper);
chess.com Rapid-equivalent $\approx$ 1820--2340; chess.com Blitz-equivalent $\approx$ 1730--2700
(ChessGoals table, interpolated). Route B (the Sunfish bot) was not run.

\paragraph{v1.0 (compiled), provisional and under-sampled.} The same instrument and the same fit, run on
64 games at UCI\_Elo 1800--2400 (16 per level; scores 84.4, 75.0, 65.6 and 56.2\,\%; zero flags)
\src{RESULTS.md, rows \code{v1.0-vs-sf*-real}}, give a maximum-likelihood CCRL-40/4-scale rating of
$\approx$ \textbf{2305}, bootstrap 95\,\% interval 2199--2426, widening to 2159--2462 once the
yardstick's own $\pm$100 calibration uncertainty is added in quadrature.

\textbf{This is quoted as provisional and should not be repeated as the build's rating.} The protocol in
this section calls for at least 60 games per level; 16 is barely a quarter of that, the sample is one
fifth of what produced the v0.2 figure, and the levels stop at 2400 while the build scores above 50\,\%
there --- which is exactly the condition under which the protocol says to extend upward, because a fit
whose top level is not a losing one is extrapolating at its own boundary. The human-scale conversions are
deliberately not computed from it. \pending{full-sample re-rating of v1.0: $\geq 60$ games per level,
levels extended above 2400 until the build scores under 50\,\%}

\paragraph{Why the ladder's anchors cannot be mapped to CCRL directly.} Five house engines hold fixed
ladder ratings and also have published CCRL Blitz (40/4) ratings. The two sets do not agree even in order.

or similar) and set the anchor values by their own calibration. We therefore do not derive a ladder-to-CCRL

\paragraph{Yardstick matches (local only).} The instrument is \code{tools/yardstick/agent.py}, a
harness-compatible agent that drives a UCI engine through \code{chess.engine.SimpleEngine} on the real
clock it is handed, with \code{Threads=1}, \code{Hash=16}, \code{Move Overhead=100},
\code{UCI\_LimitStrength=True} and \code{UCI\_Elo} at a level taken from an environment variable. The
engine is a locally installed Stockfish, whose \code{UCI\_Elo} is documented as calibrated at 120~s + 1~s
and anchored to CCRL 40/4, close to the competition clock; the same wrapper without strength options runs
the real Sunfish as a second yardstick. Levels 1400, 1600, 1800, 2000 and 2200 (extended upward if the
agent scores over 70\,\% at 2200), at least 60 games per level at 120~s + 0.5~s, colours alternated,
openings from \code{data/openings.txt}, machine load noted. Fit: the maximum-likelihood rating $R$ over all
games with expected score $1/(1 + 10^{(L - R)/400})$ against level $L$ and draws counted as one half; a
bootstrap over games for the 95\,\% interval; then the yardstick's own calibration uncertainty ($\pm 100$)
added in quadrature. Reported as ``CCRL-40/4-scale $\approx R$ (95\,\% interval \ldots)''. Alongside it:
the harness's Elo-vs-baselines numbers, the score against the real Sunfish, and, once the operator has
ladder results, the ladder rating, which pins one point of the ladder-to-CCRL map at our own strength.

\paragraph{Conversion to human scales, two independent routes.} Engine-pool Elo does not map cleanly to
human ratings; a difference of $X$ between two engines is not a difference of $X$ between two humans, and
the mapping is not linear, so two routes are used and the union of their ranges is quoted.
\emph{Route A (engine pool to FIDE)}: from the CCRL-scale $R$, the TalkChess rule of thumb
$\mathrm{FIDE} \approx 2800 - 0.7 \times (2800 - \mathrm{CCRL})$ as the upper end (it is stated to break
down below about 1800 CCRL) and $\mathrm{FIDE} \approx \mathrm{CCRL} - 100$ as the lower end; low
confidence. \emph{Route B (human-anchored bot)}: the real Sunfish bot on lichess holds a rapid rating of
1808 against humans, running on PyPy with pondering; on CPython without pondering about 100 lower is
assumed, so about 1700 lichess rapid. Our measured Elo difference against Sunfish is compressed by
0.7--1.0 for the engine-to-human transfer and added to that anchor, then converted lichess rapid $\to$
FIDE and $\to$ chess.com with the ChessGoals July-2026 comparison table (about 20\,000 profiles; its FIDE
column has no reliable data below about 1550 FIDE). The chess.com Rapid and Blitz ranges come from the
same table by interpolation between these points: FIDE 1740 $\leftrightarrow$ blitz 1500 / rapid 1655;
1865 $\leftrightarrow$ 1800 / 1870; 1965 $\leftrightarrow$ 2000 / 1995; 2075 $\leftrightarrow$ 2200 /
2110; 2190 $\leftrightarrow$ 2400 / 2215; 2300 $\leftrightarrow$ 2600 / 2300. Ranges, never a point.

\paragraph{Final statement.} For the \textbf{interpreted v0.2}, on 300 games: CCRL-scale $\approx$ 2050
(1940--2170); FIDE-equivalent $\approx$ 1840--2360; chess.com Rapid-equivalent $\approx$ 1820--2340;
chess.com Blitz-equivalent $\approx$ 1730--2700. These are estimates built on (i) a rating-limited
reference engine, (ii) one human-rated bot and (iii) a survey-based table; the honest uncertainty is
$\pm 200$ or more on the human scales.

For the \textbf{shipped v1.0}, no rating is claimed. The provisional 64-game fit above puts it near
CCRL-scale 2300, and the direct comparison against v0.2 --- +560 Elo over 300 games, an interval far from
zero --- says the gap is large and well measured. But an engine-pool Elo difference measured at 10~s +
0.1~s against one opponent is not a rating, the yardstick sample behind the 2300 is a quarter of the
protocol's own requirement, and no human-scale conversion is offered from it. The number a judge should
be given for this build is the one measured against known opposition: 84.4, 75.0, 65.6 and 56.2\,\% at
UCI\_Elo 1800, 2000, 2200 and 2400, sixteen games each, with the intervals shown in the v1.0 addendum.

\section{Known limitations and next stage}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Lucena.} The rook-endgame technique of \code{1K1k4/1P6/8/8/8/8/r7/2R5 w} is beyond a
        depth-6 horizon; not converted at 5~s per move \src{RESULTS.md, ``Known unconverted wins''}.
  \item \textbf{KQ vs K at halfmove clock 82.} \code{8/8/8/8/3k4/8/8/4KQ2 w - - 82 60} is winnable (mate
        in 13 plies with 18 of room) but is drawn by the fifty-move rule at 5~s per move: the engine makes
        steady progress but not at tablebase pace, and is two moves from mate when the rule lands. At the
        real clock the urgency clamps can buy time; at 5~s the hard limit caps every move at 1\,250~ms
        \src{DECISIONS.md item D}.
  \item \textbf{Private-API key, narrowed.} \code{Board.\_transposition\_key()} is private to python-chess
        and is still called in \code{search.py}. It is safe at the pinned 1.11.2, and it no longer runs
        during a rated game --- the compiled engine uses our own Zobrist keys --- but the code ships.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Limitations of the shipped v1.0.} The Stage 0 list above is kept because it is the history of
the build; most of it was closed by the compiled engine. Two of the three unconverted endgames are now
won, the speed ceiling is gone, and the speed factor and time constants have been measured against the
platform. What remains, in order of how much it is likely to cost:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{No king-danger term in the evaluation.} The only king-safety term is a count of pawns in
        front of the king; there is no attacker count, no weighting by attacking piece and no penalty for
        an open file bearing on our own king. Rated round 70 was lost to exactly this: the engine castled
        into a queen already bearing on the castled position, and later scored the losing
        \code{22\ldots Nxd4} at $+305$ for itself where Stockfish had the position at $-834$. Replaying
        it at up to sixteen times the time it actually spent, and at four to five plies deeper on the
        compiled engine, changes none of the three losing decisions --- so it is not a horizon failure
        that depth dissolves, but a gap in what the evaluation knows to look for. Analysis by Yan
        Beletskiy \src{handoff/FINDING-king-safety.md}. This was the highest-value evaluation work
        outstanding and the one whose absence is visible in a lost game rather than in a hunch.

        \emph{Status.} A king-danger term counting attackers into the king's zone has since been
        written and is on the development branch, gated on the parity comparison against the Python
        evaluation like every other term \src{DECISIONS.md, 2026-09-08, ``A king-danger term''}. It is
        \textbf{not in v1.0}, which is the build this report describes and the build that has played,
        and it has not yet won a promotion match, so nothing is claimed for it here.
  \item \textbf{Start-up time varies by fifteen seconds, and the sample is two.} The two platform smoke
        games compiled in 34.8~s and 49.4~s on identical hardware. The budget is 90~s and the warm-up
        deadline arms at 70~s; a run slower than the worse of the two starts skipping phases and finishes
        compiling on the first move. See the v1.0 addendum.
  \item \textbf{The evaluation is an untuned prior.} Texel tuning was attempted and \emph{rejected on
        measurement}: the tuned weights lost 100 Elo over 300 games, and a regularised second attempt
        still lost 23 with the interval touching zero \src{RESULTS.md, rows \code{v0.3-texel-vs-v0.2-10s}
        and \code{v0.3-texel-lambda40-vs-v0.2-10s}}. The prior therefore stands, which is an honest
        negative result rather than an untried idea.
  \item \textbf{One endgame on a knife edge.} \code{8/8/8/3K4/8/8/8/q6k b - - 0 291} needs 19 of the 19
        plies left before the 600-ply cap; it was mated exactly at ply 600 in one replay and drawn in
        another. Which side of the line a run lands on depends on the wall clock, so it is claimed as
        neither a fix nor a regression \src{RESULTS.md, ``the recorded unconverted wins''}.
  \item \textbf{v1.0 has no full-sample rating} (Section~\ref{sec:rating}): 16 games per level against
        the yardstick, against a protocol that asks for 60, and the top level is one the build still wins.
  \item \textbf{One platform run.} Everything known about this build on the competition hardware comes
        from a single validation, two smoke games of 20 plies. Every rated round is another sample and the
        \code{init} and per-move lines of each are read.
\end{itemize}
\paragraph{Stage 1 is done.} It was defined as: perft agreement with python-chess to depth 4 on the
standard positions and depth 3 on every collected opening and $\geq 2\,000$ random positions; identical
legal-move sets on $\geq 100\,000$ random positions; Stage 0's fuzz and property tests passing unchanged;
import time from the zip $\leq 40$~s; and $\geq 300$ games against the previous version with the 95\,\%
interval entirely above zero \src{brief, \S 5}. All of those are met: the gates are in
Section~\ref{sec:testing}, import from the zip is 18.5~s, and the match was +560 Elo over 300 games with
the interval far from zero.

\paragraph{What is next, in the order the evidence ranks it.} First, \textbf{king danger} --- the one
outstanding gap with a lost game behind it rather than a hunch, and the reason round 70 was lost. Second,
\textbf{time management}: the platform's own logs show per-move times falling into two lumps, an iteration
that finished early or one that ran to the hard ceiling, with nothing between, and the divisor that shares
the clock across the game is wrong in both directions --- a 23-move game ended with 69\,\% of its clock
unspent while a 113-move game finished on 4.7~s. Third, \textbf{a full-sample re-rating} of the shipped
build.

Both of the first two are now written and sit on the development branch --- the timing rule of
Section~\ref{sec:time} and the king-danger term above. \textbf{Neither has yet won a promotion match
against v1.0}, and under the rule this project runs on, a change that has not won one is not a version.
Until that match is played and its interval is above zero, v1.0 remains the build, and the numbers in this
report are its numbers. Recording that plainly matters more than the two changes do: the rule is only
worth anything if it also binds the changes we happen to believe in. Not on the list: Texel tuning, which was tried and lost; and playing for the opponent's flag, which
was investigated by Yan Beletskiy and rejected on arithmetic --- with a 0.5~s increment the opponent's
measured net drain was 0.063~s a move, so flagging from 10~s would take about 157 moves and the referee's
600-ply draw lands first \src{handoff/FINDING-flagging.md}. Both are recorded as negative results rather
than dropped, because the reason not to do them is the useful part.


\section*{Addendum: build v0.2}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Addendum: build v0.2}
After the Stage 0 build was frozen, the search and evaluation were extended within the same
python-chess architecture (the compiled Stage 1 engine was deferred). v0.2 adds, each behind a
plain constant so it can be bisected: null-move pruning (minimum depth 3, $R = 2 + d/6$, never in
check, only with non-pawn material, with a static-evaluation guard), late-move reductions (quiet
moves after the first three at depth $\geq 3$, reduced by one ply and re-searched on a fail-high),
aspiration windows at the root from depth 4 ($\pm 40$ centipawns, widened fourfold on failure),
futility pruning of quiet moves at depths 1--2 (margins 150/300), delta pruning in quiescence
(margin 200), staged move generation, and evaluation terms for passed, doubled and isolated pawns,
the bishop pair, rooks on open and semi-open files and the middlegame king pawn shield. Every new
constant is a hand-chosen textbook-magnitude value recorded in \code{docs/PROVENANCE.md}. Nodes to
depth 6 from the standard start fell from 238\,000 to 17\,000; in three seconds the engine now
reaches depth 9 where v0.1 reached depth 6 \src{DECISIONS.md, 2026-09-07 v0.2 entries}.

\section*{Addendum: build v1.0, the compiled engine}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Addendum: build v1.0, the compiled engine}
Stage 1 was built and shipped. The board, move generation, evaluation and search were rewritten onto a
0x88 mailbox representation with integer-coded moves and compiled by numba at import; the algorithms are
those of Section~\ref{sec:search} and Section~\ref{sec:eval}, unchanged in substance. python-chess remains
in \code{agent.py} as the legality oracle and the fallback, so the safety argument of
Section~\ref{sec:safety} is untouched: the compiled search proposes, and a python-chess legality check
disposes.

\paragraph{What stayed in Python, and why.} The iterative-deepening root, the aspiration window and the
root move loop are Python; only \code{negamax} crosses into compiled code, once per root move per
iteration. The boundary costs 4.0~µs per crossing, which is negligible at a handful of crossings per move
and ruinous at millions --- and compiling the root as well was measured at 34.2~s of extra start-up for no
gain \src{RESULTS.md, ``Stage 1 phase 2'' tables}. Two constants changed as a direct consequence of
compilation: a clock read from inside jitted code costs 301~ns through \code{numba.objmode} rather than
about 60~ns, so \code{NODE\_CHECK\_INTERVAL} is 512 in the compiled search where it is 128 in the Python
one --- the same wall-clock granularity at thirteen times the node rate.

\paragraph{The oracle.} The Python evaluation is the specification and the compiled one is a port of it,
so \code{tests/test\_fasteval.py} requires the two to agree \emph{integer for integer} --- there is no
rounding to excuse a difference, and one centipawn of drift would change which move the search picks. The
gate compares 2\,000 positions drawn from playouts of the 219 curated opening positions by default, and
20\,000 when \code{LETAL\_FULL\_GATES=1}, which is what is run before anything is promoted; it also
compares all 219 openings exactly, a hand-built position for each evaluation term, and every position
against its colour-swapped mirror, where an indexing error in the Black tables would show up.

Two things this settles that the smoke games could not. **The hard deadline holds on the platform.** Two
of the seventy moves exceeded it, both by exactly 1~ms --- the clock is read every 512 nodes, so a
millisecond is the granularity of the measurement rather than a breach. Across a full rated game the
abort path did what Section~\ref{sec:safety} claims for it. And **the soft budget does not hold, by
design and by a wide margin**: 19 of 70 moves ran past it into the hard window. That is the bimodal
spending Section~\ref{sec:time} describes, measured on the shipped build in a rated game, and it is the
defect the successor's iteration control targets. That is the only platform run of the compiled build to
date; every other platform figure in this report is the interpreted engine and is labelled as such.

\paragraph{The start-up risk, stated plainly.} numba compilation is paid once, at import, inside the 90~s
budget and before the clock starts. In those two smoke games it took 34.8~s and 49.4~s, for total start-up
times of 35.8~s and 50.7~s. Both completed every warm-up phase, so the degraded path --- in which the
warm-up runs out of its 70~s budget and the remaining compilation is finished on the first move --- has
never yet run outside a test. The mean is comfortable; \textbf{the variance is the risk}. Identical code on
identical hardware started up fifteen seconds apart, and a run slower than the worse of the two begins
skipping phases. Two samples is not a distribution, so the \code{init} line of every rated log is watched
as a further sample. This is the largest open risk in the build and it is why the warm-up deadline exists.

\section{Provenance of every constant}\label{app:provenance}
Copied from two files: \code{docs/PROVENANCE.md}, the human-readable record, and
\code{weights/PROVENANCE.json}, the machine-readable one, which is the copy that \textbf{ships in
the zip}. The brief requires both to cover every parameter -- piece values, piece-square tables,
evaluation weights, the search margins (null-move, the LMR settings, futility, aspiration, delta),
the time-management constants and the transposition-table size. The shipped JSON carries 44 such
rows; \code{docs/} never ships, so it is the JSON that answers the question a judge is most likely
to ask about a constant, and it is complete rather than restricted to the tables. The generator
commit is \code{4109710} (full hash in the JSON).

\textbf{What identifies these tables is the content hash, not the commit stamp.}
\code{weights/pst.json} records \code{\_provenance.tables\_sha256} over the tables themselves, and
that is the figure to check: it changes if and only if a number changes. The
\code{git\_commit} beside it records which \emph{run} of the generator last wrote the file, which is
a weaker thing --- re-running \code{gen\_pst.py} after an unrelated change re-stamps it with
whatever \code{HEAD} then is, on tables that did not move. The two are easy to confuse and only one
of them is evidence. Anyone verifying this should re-run the generator and compare the tables, not
the file: the tables reproduce bit for bit, and we have checked that they do at the commit named
above.
