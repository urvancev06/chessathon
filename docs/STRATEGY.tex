\begin{keybox}
\textbf{In one paragraph.} Our engine is uploaded, safe, and the strongest we have shipped. We
lose games for one reason: it misjudges positions and walks into losses believing it is winning.
Tonight we tested our biggest idea for fixing that --- a neural network --- and it lost by 67 Elo.
Research then told us a second attempt has about a 7\% chance, and pointed instead at
\textbf{six changes worth roughly 100 Elo between them, every one with a measured figure from a
comparable engine.} That is the plan for the final 36 hours. Winning outright is not realistic.
A strong finish in Friday's tournament is.
\end{keybox}

\section{The core problem}

\subsection{What three traced games showed}

\textbf{Round 85 (lost).} From move 14 to move 36 --- 23 consecutive moves --- our engine believed
it was winning by one to two pawns. It was losing by two to six. \textbf{The sign was wrong on 21
of 23 moves.} Not a blunder and not time trouble: move 13 was searched \textbf{11 moves deep over
812,000 positions} and returned ``+45, I am fine'' for a position scored $-460$.

\textbf{Round 87 (drawn).} Same disease, but wrong \emph{in both directions in one game} ---
too pessimistic by 100--370 centipawns for twenty moves, then too optimistic by 130--340 for the
next ten. \textbf{This is the more important one}: a consistent bias could be corrected with a
constant. Noise cannot.

\textbf{Round 88 (lost).} Traded rooks into a lost pawn endgame. At move 41 it searched
\textbf{19 moves deep over 2.28 million positions} and reported $-143$ for a position that was
$-578$.

\subsection{Why more computing power cannot fix it}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{The search is only as good as the judgement at the end of it.}

The engine looks ahead, reaches positions it cannot look past, and asks its evaluation: ``is this
good for me?'' Every decision traces back to that answer.

Our evaluation counts material and where pieces sit. It has \textbf{no concept of activity} ---
no measure of how many squares your pieces control. So when we are a pawn up but the opponent has
two bishops raking open diagonals and open lines at our king, it reports \textbf{``+200,
winning.''}

Searching deeper does not fix that. It explores more thoroughly \emph{toward the wrong goal}.
\end{keybox}

This is also why a faster machine adds no strength. \textbf{The competition runs every engine on
one core of the organisers' server, 2 GB, no GPU.} We upload a zip; their machine runs it. Nothing
we own is involved on Friday. A better machine lets us \emph{test} more, which matters enormously
--- but through finding out what works, not through raw power.

\subsection{Verdict on a second attempt: no, about 7\%}

We commissioned research into how working nets are actually trained. The literature is broadly
\emph{favourable} to trying again --- and the answer is still no, for a specific reason.

\textbf{The one real deviation is data.} The working range across the field is \textbf{50--3000
training positions per parameter. We had 1.9.} The lowest volume anyone reports a working net at
is 1.5 million positions; we had 186,000. A master's thesis abandoned precisely our approach ---
online games labelled by an engine --- as ``too small for this task.''

\textbf{But that is not what cost us 67 Elo.} A starved network is one that judges positions
badly. Ours judged them \emph{better} than our hand-written evaluation on four independent
measurements, including at real search leaves, and still played 67 Elo worse.

And the three mechanisms proposed as the fix have each already been tested here and killed: the
outputs are not compressed (they are 5--19\% \emph{wider}); the error does not explode on unusual
positions (the network stays ahead there); and the improved training target is one
\textbf{the network already wins.}

\begin{keybox}
So the honest position is not ``we know what went wrong and can fix it.'' It is \textbf{``the
network was better by every measure we own and played worse, and nobody has explained why.''}
The right response to an unexplained result with 36 hours left is not to rebuild it ten times
larger.

$0.7$ (pipeline correct first time, no iteration) $\times$ $0.35$ (true effect positive at all)
$\times$ $0.3$ (an affordable test resolves it) $\approx$ \bad{7\%}.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{Three things I had wrong, corrected}

\textbf{1. ``The architecture cannot see kings, and no training fixes that.''} I called this
fatal. \textbf{768$\rightarrow$128 is what other engines beat their hand-written evaluations
with} --- one reports \good{+200 Elo} from it; another measured a width curve where \textbf{128 is
the peak} and 512 is worse. The standard training tool's author advises starting exactly there.
I invented a fatal flaw that is not one.

\textbf{2. ``We have an incremental accumulator.''} We do not. The code rebuilds it from scratch
on every call, deliberately, and says so in its own comment. I stated the opposite repeatedly.

\textbf{3. Mobility as the leading candidate --- and then a correction to the correction.}
I wrote that mobility was \emph{ruled out} as a net loss. \textbf{That overstated what we
measured, and the arithmetic was ours.} The 1.7$\times$ figure is nodes-to-depth, which is a fair
currency only when the evaluation is unchanged: the mobility build's depth 6 is not the
baseline's depth 6, so the metric prices the cost and credits none of the knowledge. It is a
\emph{price the term must beat}, not a verdict.

Running it honestly: published gains are $+27.6$, $+41 \pm 14$ and $+62$ in engines that lacked
the term, and those are net of a near-zero cost because they are bitboard engines counting an
attack set they already hold. Ours is a mailbox and has no attack sets. So our net is roughly
$(27.6 \text{ to } 62) - (60 \text{ to } 65) = $ \textbf{$-37$ to $+2$} --- break-even with wide
uncertainty. \textbf{We do not know the sign.} That is not ``ruled out'' and it is not
``comfortably worth it''.

Two ten-minute diagnostics decide it, and neither needs games: scale the term down and see
whether the tree cost falls with it, and turn reverse futility off and see whether the tree cost
disappears. \textbf{If the second one fires it is bigger than mobility} --- our reverse-futility
margin is 85 centipawns while our evaluation's median error at those same positions is 342, so
every future evaluation change would be carrying an artificial tax from a threshold that was
never coherent with our own noise.

\subsection{Why correction history is the pick}

We measured that at \emph{real search leaves} our evaluation is off by a median of \textbf{304--342
centipawns} --- about a knight --- while our pruning thresholds are 150--750. \textbf{We are making
decisions on differences smaller than our own error.}

Correction history records, for each material configuration, \textbf{how far the search's verdict
usually lands from the static evaluation}, and adds that learned offset back before those
decisions are taken. It is the only item that attacks the pathology we actually observed rather
than one we assumed.

One array lookup. It passed 128,672 games of testing in Stockfish, and it \emph{scales up} with
longer time controls --- ours is the long one.

\section{Free points --- do all of these}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{Upload the final build early Friday, not at 11:00.} Explicit tie-break; ties on
      points are common over 13 rounds.
\item \textbf{Never lose to a crash, an illegal move, or the clock.} Each is a whole point given
      away. The 500-game gate exists for this and must run on whatever ships.
\item \textbf{Keep uploading until the lock.} Ten a day, latest valid one plays, a failed
      validation costs nothing. Every rated game is a free reliability test.
\item \textbf{Fix the time allocation.} In round 87 the engine spent \textbf{1.0 second on the
      losing move while holding 88 seconds}, and 9.4 seconds on a move in an already-lost endgame,
      finishing with 32.9 unused. Spending time inversely to where it matters is a defect.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Honest assessment}

\textbf{Winning outright is not realistic.} The top of the field is measurably better at chess ---
a blunder rate of 0.0--0.9\% against our 1.8\% --- and closing that is an evaluation rebuild, not a
day of tuning.

\textbf{A strong Swiss finish is realistic and is the right target.} Thirteen rounds on points,
locked builds, ladder seeding nearly irrelevant. Consistency matters more there than peak
strength, and consistency is what a lower blunder rate buys. Ours has already halved once.

\textbf{What would have to be true for a top-five finish:}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item the test running tonight lands positive (expected $+15$ to $+25$);
\item three or four of the six changes land and stack;
\item we give away nothing to crashes, flags or lost time --- entirely in our control;
\item the Friday pairings are not brutal early --- not in our control.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{And the thing that is genuinely ours}

Every number here has a matched comparison, a stated uncertainty, and a caveat where one is owed.
\textbf{Four times today a persuasive measurement said we were winning and the games said
otherwise} --- and each time it was caught, because someone wrote down in advance what would count
as evidence.

Five separate defects today shared one shape: a check that could not fail. A test set defined
using the training data. A ``wait until the machine is free'' loop whose own command matched its
own search. A data filter that called the function under test. A guard written to catch the
incident that had already happened. A benchmark that verified how fast an answer arrived but never
that it was the same answer.

The question that catches all five, now in our decision log:

\begin{center}
\emph{What is this check defined in terms of, and is that the thing I am testing?}
\end{center}

That is what we can defend in front of a judge, and it is worth more than any single technique.
