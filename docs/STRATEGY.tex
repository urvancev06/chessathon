\section*{Summary}

We are behind, the reason is measurable, and the biggest single lever we had has just failed
a fair test. This document records what is actually established, explains why the neural
evaluation lost 67 Elo when every static indicator said it should win, and ranks everything
still available in the time remaining.

The central finding is not about the network. It is this: \textbf{our losses are evaluation
failures, not search failures.} Every improvement we have shipped so far makes the engine
search better. None of them touches the thing that actually loses us games.

\subsection{Two games traced move by move}

\textbf{Round 85} (lost, checkmate). From move 14 to move 36 our evaluation read $+100$ to
$+200$ while Stockfish had the position between $-139$ and $-674$. \textbf{The sign was wrong
on 21 of 23 consecutive moves.} It was not a blunder and not time trouble: move 13 was searched
to depth~11 over 812{,}795 nodes and entered a $-460$ position. Eleven plies did not reveal a
five-pawn error.

\textbf{Round 87} (drawn by repetition, first likely v1.2 game). Our evaluation was wrong by
100--400\,cp \emph{in both directions in the same game} --- pessimistic by 100--370 through
moves 13--29, then optimistic by 130--340 from move 30. \textbf{This is the more important
finding}, because a consistent bias could be tuned away and noise cannot.

One success: from $-722$ and seeing forced mate against it, the engine steered into a threefold
repetition and saved half a point. Repetition detection works.

\section{Why the network failed}

\subsection{The result}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
768$\rightarrow$128 network vs.\ v1.2, 300 games at 120s$+$0.5s & \\
\midrule
Score & $+111 = 21 - 168$, 40.5\% \\
Elo & \bad{$-67$} \ (95\%: $-106$ to $-29$) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\textbf{It is not the speed cost.} The network costs 14.6\% of node rate, which is 0.105 of a
ply at 4.5 nodes per depth, worth 5--9 Elo. The loss is 67. So \textbf{58--62 Elo of it is the
network choosing worse moves.} Recovering the speed would not have rescued it.

\textbf{And every static indicator said it would win.} On held-out positions it was 2.6$\times$
more accurate than the hand-crafted evaluation (16{,}503 vs.\ 43{,}039 squared error; RMSE
128\,cp vs.\ 207). On the 26 positions of the round-85 loss it identified who was winning 20
times against the hand-crafted evaluation's 5.

This is the \textbf{third time} on this project that accuracy against engine labels has pointed
the wrong way. The first was a 768-parameter Texel fit that ranked best on the same metric and
played 100 Elo worse.

\subsection{You are right that it should be better. Here is what is wrong with ours.}

A properly built NNUE is worth several hundred Elo in real engines. Ours lost 67. The gap is
not evidence that the approach fails; it is evidence that we built it wrong in six specific,
nameable ways. Ranked by how much I think each cost us:

\paragraph{1. The architecture cannot represent king safety --- and that is our actual weakness.}
Our input is 768 features: 12 piece types $\times$ 64 squares. \textbf{There is no king context
anywhere in it.} Real NNUE uses HalfKP/HalfKA, where the feature is the triple (our king's
square, piece, square) --- 40{,}960 inputs. That is what lets a network learn ``a knight on f5
is worth a great deal \emph{because} the enemy king is on g8.''

Our network is structurally incapable of learning that. And king safety is precisely the
blindness round 85 exposed: we misjudged a position because we could not see open lines at our
own king. \textbf{We built a replacement that shares the original's blind spot.} No amount of
training fixes this one.

\paragraph{2. We asked it to predict something it cannot compute.}
We labelled positions with Stockfish at \emph{depth 12}. A depth-12 score encodes tactics twelve
plies deep. Our network is a static function: it sees a position and outputs a number, with no
search. For any position whose true value depends on a tactic, \textbf{the target is
unlearnable}, and 98{,}700 parameters will fit that unlearnable component as noise. That is
overfitting by construction, and the measured train/held-out gap of 2.8$\times$ is its
signature.

Standard practice labels with a \emph{shallow} search (depth 6--9) or blends the eventual game
result, precisely so the target is something a static function can represent.

\paragraph{3. Three to four orders of magnitude too little data.}
We trained on 186{,}190 quiet positions. Serious NNUE training uses \textbf{100 million to
1 billion}. Worse, ours came from 3{,}356 games at $\sim$51 positions per game, so consecutive
samples are heavily correlated and the effective independent sample is far smaller than 186k.
Against 98{,}700 parameters that is roughly two examples per parameter.

\paragraph{4. The wrong loss function.}
We minimised squared error on raw centipawns clipped to $\pm$1500. Being 200\,cp wrong in a
level position is catastrophic; being 200\,cp wrong at $+1400$ is irrelevant --- you are winning
either way. Raw-centipawn MSE spends most of its gradient on positions that are already decided.
Standard practice trains in win-probability space through a sigmoid.

\paragraph{5. The training distribution does not match the search's leaves.}
We trained on positions from other engines' ladder games --- middlegame-heavy, drawn from actual
play. The network is asked about positions at \emph{the leaves of our own search}: often
unbalanced, deep in a tree, reached by move sequences no human or engine would choose. Standard
practice generates training data by self-play from the engine itself for exactly this reason.

\paragraph{6. It was trained to answer the wrong question.}
The deepest one. We trained a function to \emph{predict a search's verdict about a position}.
What the engine needs is a function that \emph{orders moves correctly at the leaves of its own
search}. Those are different objectives, and nothing in the pipeline optimised the second. A
network can be excellent at the first and useless at the second --- which is exactly the result
we got, and exactly what the three failures of held-out MSE have been telling us.

\subsection{What a correct second attempt looks like}

Every item above except (1) is fixable with the time and machine we have. The critical
realisation is about cost:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Self-play data generation is nearly free, because the labels come from the generating
search itself.} We do not need Stockfish, which caps at 253 positions/second. At 5{,}000 nodes
per move our engine spends $\sim$6\,ms per move; a 200-ply game costs $\sim$1.2\,s of one core.
On 14 cores that is roughly \textbf{2 million positions per hour}.
\end{quote}

So four hours of machine time yields $\sim$8 million positions --- \textbf{43$\times$ our current
dataset} --- with labels attached at no extra cost. That single change addresses causes 2, 3
and 5 simultaneously.

A realistic second attempt:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Self-play generation}, 4 hours, our own engine at a fixed small node budget,
      labels from the generating search blended with the game result.
\item \textbf{King-bucketed inputs}: not full HalfKP (40{,}960 inputs, 10.5\,MB of weights) but
      4--8 buckets by king position, giving 3{,}072--6{,}144 inputs and $\sim$1.5\,MB. This
      recovers most of cause (1) at a fraction of the cost, and stays far inside the 50\,MB cap.
\item \textbf{Sigmoid/WDL loss} instead of raw-centipawn MSE.
\item \textbf{Shallow labels}, so the target is learnable.
\end{enumerate}

Estimated cost: 4h generation $+$ 0.5h training $+$ 2.4h screening $\approx$ \textbf{7 hours of
the machine}. That is over half of Thursday.

After the screen we built a fifth check designed specifically to escape the circularity ---
evaluations scored against \emph{the actual result of the game}, on 12{,}000 held-out quiet

\textbf{The network won this too.} Four independent metrics have now ranked first the build that
lost 67 Elo: held-out error against depth-12 labels, a sign test against depth-18, error on quiet

\textbf{The only instrument that has ever been right about playing strength on this project is
playing games.} That governs everything below. A second network attempt cannot be checked as it
is built: it costs $\sim$7 hours to make and a 2.4-hour screen to evaluate, with no cheap proxy
and no way to iterate. That materially lowers its expected value, and this document overstated it
before this result arrived.

\subsection{The one mechanism that fits every observation}

Exactly one hypothesis explains ``better at judging positions, worse at playing'':
\textbf{the network is asked about positions it was never trained on.}

Training data is real-game positions. A search asks about \emph{leaf nodes} --- unbalanced,
deep in a tree, reached by move sequences no engine would choose. A hand-crafted evaluation
degrades gracefully out there because material still counts. \textbf{A network's error off its
training distribution is unbounded.}

If true, this changes the design of any second attempt: training data must be sampled from
\emph{inside search trees}, not from games. That is a different job from the self-play plan in
Section 2.3, and it is being tested now.

\section{Everything we can do, ranked}

\subsection{Free points --- do all of these}

These cost almost no machine time and are not conditional on any measurement.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Submit the final build early on Friday, not at 11:00.} ``Earlier final
      submission'' is an explicit tie-break. In a 13-round Swiss, ties on points are common.
      This is free.
\item \textbf{Never lose a game to a crash, illegal move or flag.} Every such loss is a whole
      point given away for nothing. Our 500-game fuzz gate exists for this; run it on whatever
      we finally ship.
\item \textbf{Upload early and often.} Ten uploads a day, the latest valid one plays, a failed
      validation costs nothing. Every rated round between now and the lock is a free sample of
      our own reliability.
\item \textbf{Fix the time allocation.} Round 87 spent \textbf{1.0\,s on the losing move with
      88\,s on the clock}, and 9.4\,s on a move in an already-lost endgame. It finished with
      32.9\,s unused. Spending time inversely to where it matters is a defect, it is cheap to
      investigate, and it costs no Elo to fix.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{What we should not do}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A faster development machine will not make the engine stronger.} The competition
      runs on one core of an AMD EPYC 9V74 with 2\,GB, identical for everyone. A VPS helps us
      \emph{measure} in parallel --- worth having, since we now have more candidates than slots
      --- but it cannot add a single Elo on Friday.
\item \textbf{Do not trust held-out accuracy against engine labels again.} It has pointed the
      wrong way three times on this project. Only games count.
\item \textbf{Do not bundle an evaluation change with a search change.} A flat result then loses
      both candidates at once with no way to attribute, on the last day.
\end{itemize}

\section{Honest assessment}

\textbf{Winning outright is not realistic.} The top of the field is measurably better at chess
than we are --- 0.0--0.9\% blunder rate against our 1.8\% --- and that gap is an evaluation
rebuild, not a weekend of tuning.

\textbf{A strong Swiss finish is realistic and is the right target.} Thirteen rounds decided on
points, over locked builds, with our ladder seeding almost irrelevant. Consistency matters more
than peak strength there, and consistency is exactly what a lower blunder rate buys. Our blunder
rate has already halved once.

\textbf{What would have to be true for a top-five finish:} the ordering bundle lands near
$+25$; mobility or a corrected network cuts the blunder rate meaningfully below 1.5\%; we give
away nothing to crashes, flags or time; and the Swiss pairings are kind in the early rounds. The
first is likely, the second is uncertain, the third is entirely in our control, and the fourth
is not.

\textbf{The single most valuable thing we own is not a technique.} It is that every number in
this document has a matched baseline, a stated interval, and a caveat attached where one is
owed. Three times a persuasive metric told us we were winning and the games said otherwise, and
each time it was caught because someone wrote down beforehand what would count as evidence.
